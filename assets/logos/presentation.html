This is where the ambigram has got to. Below is how it got here, what I changed from your sketch and why, and the two bits that honestly do not work yet.

The briefyour words

What you asked for

The basic idea is that this will be able to rotate 180° around the center “O”. Its not something you’ll look at cold and recognize it as the word “Motoproponent”.

The main motivation for the ambigram is to be able to paint it on a helmet as the center stripe and it be the same from either side of my helmet.

Two other things you flagged: the circles were a bit squashed, and the whole thing was more crammed together than it needed to be. Both are fixed. You also wanted a coloured line down the middle, and a line running across the top and bottom—more on those.

The problem13 letters

Why this is hard

Motoproponent has thirteen letters. Spin it round and the first letter lands where the last one was, the second lands where the twelfth was, and so on. So each letter has to be a single shape that your eye accepts as two different letters depending on which way up it is. The middle o is the only one that stays put— that is the pivot.

Some of those pairings are friendly. Some are genuinely impossible, and no amount of drawing fixes them:

  • m ↔ tworks
  • o ↔ na compromise
  • t ↔ eworks well
  • o ↔ na compromise
  • p ↔ oworks
  • r ↔ pimpossible
  • othe pivot

That is the honest picture: four of the six pairings behave, and two fight back. Which is fine, because you already said it is not meant to be readable cold.

Wrong turncapitals

Capital letters were a dead end

This was the first direction, and it is worth showing because the reason it failed is the reason the current one works.

Capitals are all one height—nothing sticks up, nothing hangs down. That leaves nowhere to hide the differences between two letters that have to share a shape. M and T both need a tall straight edge in the same spot and end up fighting over it. Worse, a capital O already looks identical upside down, so it can never turn into an N— the pair collapses into one shape that reads as a zero with a line through it.

You can also see what the top and bottom lines do here: they close the whole thing into a box.

The fixsmall letters

Small letters solved most of it

Small letters have tall bits and low bits—the stalk on a t, the tail hanging off a p. Turn the word upside down and a tall bit becomes a low bit. That is exactly the trade the difficult pairs needed, and capitals simply do not have it.

It sorted out your coloured line too. You asked for one line that is both the middle bar of the e and the top bar of the t. In capitals that is impossible—those two letters swap places when you spin it, so a line at the top of one is at the bottom of the other. A small t has its crossbar halfway up anyway, so one straight line through the middle is now both bars at once, exactly as you described it.

What happened
They backfired. Each n is really an o with a small bite taken out of the bottom, and that bite is the only thing telling your eye it is an n. The bottom line ran straight through the bite and filled it in, so every n turned back into an o.

Latestwhat changed

The three changes in the current one

It is now equally good both ways up. Every earlier attempt was drawn to look its best the right way up, so the first half read beautifully and the second half was a mess. On a helmet that means one side of your head looks right and the other looks wrong. Each letter has been pulled back toward the middle so the whole word is evenly half-readable instead. It reads as a pattern that resolves once you know the word—which is what you described wanting.

Much heavier line. Paint, vinyl, stitching and a tiny profile picture all need weight behind them. The thin version disappeared.

The top and bottom lines belong to a letter now. You described the last t’s bar, which is also part of the first m, stretched right across. In the earlier attempts I had just drawn two loose lines, which is why they caged the thing in. Now the m has a foot, and that foot is what extends —turn it upside down and it lands exactly where the final t needs its crossbar. They also stop a bit past the middle instead of running the full width, so the mark is open at two corners rather than boxed.

Second marksmall sizes

You need a small version too

Thirteen letters in a row is long and thin. Perfect down the middle of a helmet—useless as a YouTube profile picture, a favicon, a sticker or a stitched patch, which the channel will need. Shrink the full wordmark and it turns into a smudge.

So the middle o does double duty. It is the point the whole thing spins around, it is the only letter that comes out completely clean, and with the red line through it, it still reads at the size of a browser tab. It is not a second logo—it is a close-up of the one you already have.

Straight upwhat is broken

What still does not work

The r and the p will never both be right. The arm of an r sticks out to the right. The belly of a p also sticks out to the right. Spin the word and right becomes left, so one of them is always facing the wrong way. There is no clever drawing that gets round this— it is the geometry. I have drawn it to favour the r, because your eye fills in the p from the rest of the word more easily than the other way round.

The o and the n are a compromise, not a solution. The bite that turns an o into an n has no right size: small enough to keep a convincing o is too small to make a convincing n, and big enough for the n turns the o into a u. It sits between the two. Giving the letters flat sides rather than round ones helped more than fiddling with the bite did.

Neither of these stops the mark working the way you described it. They just mean it will never be a thing someone reads cold, and I would rather you heard that from me now than noticed it later.
